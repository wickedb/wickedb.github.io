# Some thoughts on the beginning of infinity

The Beginning of Infinity presents David Deutsch's ambitious argument that explanations are the key to solving all problems not forbidden by the laws of physics. Unlike his previous work 'The Fabric of Reality', Deutsch here offers a unifying theory connecting epistemology, aesthetics, morality, justice, science, and biology. The book is widely celebrated in rationalist and tech communities as a grand philosophical synthesis that could guide humanity toward unlimited progress.

The main intention of this critique is to open up a dialectic around the Beginning of Infinity and examine the difficulties around establishing one. I start by examining Deutsch’s limits while explaining complex adaptive systems, transition into how this fallacy transfers to his narrative of history, and finally present a lens - the attractor - to examine why his framework is hard to escape. 

To preface our discussion, in the Beginning of Infinity Deutsch often seems to change domains abruptly - he transitions from talking about scientific domains (stars/evolutionary biology/mathematical abstractions) to complex-adaptive domains (social institutions/culture/morality) in leaps. I will link to a section (Appendix 1) with a collection of quotations from the Beginning of Infinity that are what I call the Slipping Points - the exact sentence in a chapter where he stops sound science-talk and slips into generalizations about culture and history. Although after chapter 9, this is hard to do because everything is muddled. We will deal with the last few chapters in section 2 of the critique. 

Apart from this, Deutsch’s epistemological core in the Beginning of Infinity is ultimately Popperian - falsifiability, criticism, and error correction - all of which I whole-heartedly agree with. So I will not be arguing against the importance of explanatory knowledge or criticism. A critique of Deutsch is not necessarily a critique of Popper. 

# Section 1: Seeking better explanations (for Complex Adaptive Systems)

> “Outside of physics, and generally in complex domains, the reasons behind things have had a tendency to make themselves less obvious to us...” - Nassim Taleb, Antifragile

I will never understand how someone can be a Deutsch and Taleb fan at once, without reconciling the fundamental differences between them. There is a difference between the explanation OF a complex-adaptive system and explanation WITHIN a complex adaptive system. Deutsch conflates these when discussing society, culture, and politics, applying explanatory frameworks that work for physical analysis to domains where emergence and opacity dominate. 

## The word ‘explanation’

Deutsch uses "explanation" uniformly across physics and politics, but explanatory success means different things in different domains. 

In physics, a good explanation reveals underlying mechanisms that enable prediction and control. In complex adaptive systems, Taleb shows us that knowledge often involves understanding structural properties - fragility, asymmetric consequences, black swans - while accepting fundamental limits on prediction and control.

In this section, I argue that Taleb’s explanations are better than Deutsch’s explanations for complex-adaptive systems. Even if Taleb’s ideas are no silver bullet, for my purposes I only need them to be better explanations than Deutsch’s. I will focus on Deutsch’s discussions on politics (chapter 9 discusses policies and chapter 13 discuss governance), social institutions (chapter 15 discusses static/dynamic societies), and history. 

## Deutsch vs Taleb: on prediction 

According to Herbert Simon, human systems - institutions, markets, societies - are near-decomposable hierarchical structures whose behavior resists the kind of unified explanations Deutsch seeks. Within such systems, Simon shows we must adopt "procedural rationality" - reasoning that adapts to constraints rather than pursuing logical optimization. This directly contradicts Deutsch's vision of universal rationality converging on objective truths. 

Taleb agrees: prediction within complex adaptive systems isn't just difficult, it's systematically misleading (the turkey problem)[[7]](#ref7). He critiques the naive rationalism of being confident in the predictive power of models and theories in systems characterized by non-linearity, opacity, and ‘rare’ black swan events. 

In his view, this obsession with prediction in such systems is not only futile, but harmful - as it breeds fragility by encouraging over-optimization or harmful-intervention. Deutsch is careful to distinguish between prophecies and prediction, but he still treats prediction in complex systems as fundamentally the same enterprise as in physics - just harder. This leads him to theorize about societies and institutions like he’s in physics class. 

## Deutsch vs Taleb: on problem solving and intervention

This is one of their main differences. Deutsch treats all progress as a chain of problems and solutions - keep solving problems, live with the solution, and solve the newer (better) problems that emerge. But Taleb forces us to ask deeper questions: which problems should be solved and which interventions risk causing more harm than good? Which problems should remain unsolved? Taleb constantly warns about the unintended damage of naive interventions that create fragility instead of progress. Deutsch’s model treats every unsolved problem as an open invitation for intervention, but in domains like economics, governance, or ecology, this approach creates what Taleb calls ‘asymmetric consequences’. 

The people (or institutions) who benefit from a solution are often different from those who bear the costs of the new problems the solution creates. [[2]](#ref2) Deutsch glosses over the crucial moral dimension: who lives the solution, and who lives with its consequences? In complex adaptive systems, the question isn’t whether the problems are soluble, but whether the act of solving them creates more fragility, and how the costs and benefits are distributed across time and populations. Deutsch's optimistic rationalism has no framework for recognizing when intervention itself is the problem.

## Deutsch and his social theories

Consider how this plays out in Deutsch's treatment of political progress. He frames political progress as fundamentally developing better explanations that can withstand rational criticism, similar to how science advances. In chapter 13, Deutsch lists “..that slavery is an abomination, or that women should be free to go out to work, or that autopsies should be legal, or that promotion in the armed forces should not depend on skin colour”[[3]](#ref3) in the context of “convergence in the broad consensus over time is made possible by the fact that all concerned are gradually eliminating errors in their positions and converging on objective truths”[[3]](#ref3). Yet this epistemological framework is misapplied to political change. All the changes he listed required sustained political struggle, activism, legal challenges, and institutional pressure - often against opponents who understood the moral arguments perfectly well but had material interests in maintaining the status quo. 

For example, Deutsch's framework cannot explain that the British colonization of India and the ‘British enlightenment’ happened simultaneously. The administrators simultaneously championed individual liberty at home while justifying paternalistic control in India. This wasn't inconsistency - they had perfectly coherent explanations for both positions. The problem is that Deutsch's framework provides no way to distinguish between genuine moral progress and sophisticated rationalization of power structures. From a Talebian perspective, their "explanations" were locally adaptive - they served the revealed preferences of their social position while maintaining intellectual coherence. The strength of democratic institutions lies not in their capacity to generate consensus around superior explanations, but in their ability to manage persistent disagreement and adapt to changing power relations without systemic collapse. 

This misunderstanding of how democratic progress actually works is part of a broader pattern in Deutsch's social theory. His framework divides societies into static versus dynamic categories[[5]](#ref5), rather than fragile versus antifragile ones. To invoke Popper here, while closed societies tend to be more stable or "static" in their social order, Popper's distinction is more about openness to critical scrutiny and reform than mere pace of change[[4]](#ref4). Open societies are inherently more complex and unstable because they allow and encourage change and dissent, which can produce anxiety but also progress. But it doesn't matter if a society is static or dynamic - what matters is whether it is fragile or not. A dynamic society that is fragile will collapse in the face of rapid change, and a static society that is antifragile will thrive off unexpected events. Instead of allowing for any nuance, Deutsch treats societies like closed systems that are either "on" or "off" in terms of creativity and adaptability. More on this in section 2.

## Deutsch vs Taleb: on individual rationality and heuristics

The problem deepens when we examine how Deutsch thinks individuals should operate within these systems. Deutsch's theory of individual rationality reveals another point where Deutsch-Taleb compatibility breaks down. Deutsch argues that "To choose an option rationally, is to choose the associated explanation. Therefore, rational decision-making consists not of weighing evidence but of explaining it." [[3]](#ref3) Surely, he is not suggesting human decision making is rational in practice (refer thinking fast and slow[[6]](#ref6)), so a likely defense here is that Deutsch isn’t claiming people actually are rational in politics, but rather describing how they should be. 

Even granting that Deutsch proposes a normative ideal rather than describing actual behavior, his framework fundamentally misunderstands what makes decision-making effective in complex adaptive systems. In environments characterized by emergence, non-linearity, and rapid change, explanations quickly become obsolete or misleading. Deutsch sees heuristics as "parochial" - temporary scaffolds that must be transcended by universal explanations. This perspective enables him to retroactively frame entire ‘failed civilizations’ as victims of heuristic thinking - a move that misjudges heuristics as primitive rather than adaptive. Through Taleb’s lens, however, heuristics are the highest-functioning form of knowledge in complex domains as they have evolved through collective trial and error. The green lumber fallacy shows that successful navigation of complex systems often requires less explanation, not more. 

Deutsch universalizes an epistemological framework that may work for scientific inquiry but fails catastrophically when applied to domains where speed, adaptability, and emergent learning trump explanatory depth. A normative theory of rationality that systematically devalues what actually works in complex environments isn't offering an ideal worth pursuing. Deutsch wants the authority of making empirical claims about how political systems actually function (chapter 13: this is the context he’s discussing ‘choices’ in), but when those claims are challenged, he retreats to normative territory. 

## Deutsch vs Taleb: closing thoughts

The apparent compatibility between Deutsch and Taleb exists only at the level of Popperian fallibilism - error correction and trial-and-error learning. While Deutsch's error correction seeks to eliminate randomness and uncertainty through better explanations, Taleb's error correction embraces randomness as information and builds antifragile systems that benefit from stressors. The issue isn't that Deutsch ignores complexity entirely—he acknowledges emergence and abstractions within scientific domains. The problem is that his explanatory ideals, while powerful for physical systems, become systematically misleading when applied to domains governed by different structural principles. 

This is why Deutsch's historical narrative from Athens to Renaissance Italy to modern science exemplifies what Taleb calls the "narrative fallacy" - imposing retrospective coherence on complex phenomena that resist linear explanation. Deutsch achieves his neat story by systematically ignoring alternative trajectories, failed civilizations with "good" explanations, and successful societies that thrived through apparent irrationality. We will deal with his story in the next section. 

# Section 2: A Physicist's Bad History

Even if Deutsch avoids determinism, he still imposes a retrospective, linear causality onto history, flattening its complexity into a neat tale of progress, all stemming from a singular spark he calls "the Enlightenment."

## The word ‘Enlightenment’

Firstly, his definition of the Enlightenment is itself easy to vary. Consider the following disclaimers from his book: “In my terminology, the continental Enlightenment understood that problems are soluble but not that they are inevitable, while the British Enlightenment understood both equally.” “Note that this is a classification of ideas, not of nations or even individual thinkers.” “In this book, when I refer to ‘the’ Enlightenment I mean the ‘British’ one.” [[8]](#ref8) This maneuver allows Deutsch to retroactively assign “Enlightenment values” to any figure or moment he approves of - whether it’s Babbage and Lovelace, Renaissance Italy, or ancient Athens - regardless of their historical context. By continually redefining the Enlightenment to suit his thesis, he gives the impression that all human progress can be traced back to a single intellectual lineage. He can just call all the exceptions  ‘mini-enlightenments’ later. 

## Deutsch’s ideas about hunter-gatherer societies

In chapter 15, he dismisses hunter-gatherer societies as inherently "static societies," arguing that “primitive societies (including tribes of hunter-gatherers) must all have been static societies, because if ever one ceased to be static it would soon cease to be primitive, or else destroy itself by losing its distinctive knowledge.”[[5]](#ref5) His own theory depends on what he calls "a tradition of criticism" as the key to sustained progress. If we were to consider recent scholarship, according to David Graeber and David Wengrow in The Dawn of Everything, the ideas about liberty, personal freedom, and critique of hierarchies that Deutsch celebrates didn't originate in Renaissance Italy's "smouldering" spark, but from European contact with indigenous North American cultures that had long-standing traditions of debate. 

These societies had developed sophisticated political sensibilities through exactly the kind of critical discourse Deutsch claims was missing from pre-Enlightenment civilization: "not only did the North Americans manage almost entirely to sidestep the evolutionary trap that we assume must always lead, eventually, from agriculture to the rise of some all-powerful state or empire; but in doing so they developed political sensibilities that were ultimately to have a deep influence on Enlightenment thinkers and, through them, are still with us today."[[9]](#ref9) The very spark Deutsch traces to Florence may have indigenous origins - from societies he categorically dismisses as "static." Even if the wengrow/graeber scholarship is not settled, and even if Deutsch can easily co-opt the Wendats into his Enlightenment tradition - I am merely pointing out how easy it is to vary his historical landscape. More on this in section 3. 

## Deutsch on Athens and its contemporaries 

Another example is in Chapter 9, when Deutsch contrasts Athens with Sparta to highlight the virtues of early democracy. Before analyzing Pericles’ praise of Athens, he adds a qualifier: “in assessing the optimism of a civilization, what that civilization aspired to be must be even more important than what it had yet succeeded in becoming.” This conveniently lets him ignore the social realities of Athens in 570-480 BCE, and focus on Pericles’ rhetoric, which is necessary because Athens was no cradle of freedom: it practiced chattel slavery, enforced veiling for women, and forbade them from appearing in public. Deutsch may counter that Athens would have eventually corrected these flaws thanks to its tradition of criticism. But why compare Athens and Sparta at all? Why not Athens and the Achaemenid Empire? While Athens advanced limited political democracy (for 10% of the population), Cyrus pioneered imperial tolerance and human rights, as reflected in the Cyrus Cylinder. Because Persia was a monarchy, Deutsch excludes it from his schema of progress, ignoring that people under Cyrus may have enjoyed more practical freedoms than Athenian citizens. [[10]](#ref10)

## Deutsch and the smouldering spark in Renaissance Italy

Similarly, while discussing Renaissance Italy's "mini-enlightenment," Deutsch notes how "they sent agents all over the known world to obtain copies of ancient books." He conveniently omits that these copies were preserved and expanded by Byzantine and Islamic scholars during the Islamic Golden Age. Scholars in Baghdad, Córdoba, and Cairo didn't just safeguard classical knowledge—they expanded it in mathematics, medicine, astronomy, and philosophy, often motivated by religious imperatives. But that doesn’t fit Deutsch’s narrative, where progress begins only when religion is cast off and Western rationalism takes over. His framework depends on a Church-vs-science dynamic, which simply didn’t exist in cultures without a church-state divide. In those contexts, knowledge and faith weren’t in conflict. [[11]](#ref11)

## Deutsch’s thoughts on historical research and rigor

This reveals how Deutsch's historical narrative is designed to be irrefutable. Present him with Islamic scholars who developed rigorous explanatory frameworks in mathematics, optics, and experimental method, and he'll respond that they lacked the right political institutions. Show him Indigenous societies that created sustainable institutions for criticism and debate, and he'll point out they were eventually snuffed - destroyed by colonizers. Demonstrate that the Achaemenids created sophisticated explanatory approaches to governance and tolerance, and he'll argue that Athens would have gotten there eventually through its tradition of criticism. Each historical counterexample can be dismissed on different grounds, while any supportive evidence proves his thesis. This isn't just methodologically problematic - it renders his entire account of human progress immune to historical refutation.

Deutsch also systematically avoids specificity when condemning past civilizations. From chapter 9: "As we look back on failed civilizations of the past, we can see that they were so poor, their technology was so feeble, and their explanations of the world so fragmentary and full of misconceptions that their caution about innovation and progress was as perverse as expecting a blindfold to be useful when navigating dangerous waters." This sweeping condemnation names no specific societies, time periods, or evidence. The vagueness is deliberate - it renders his theory hard-to-vary (in the wrong way). Any civilization that contradicts his narrative (like the Achaemenid Empire's tolerance or Islamic Baghdad's scientific achievements) can be retroactively classified as a "mini-enlightenment that was snuffed out tragically," while any that fits his story becomes proof of his thesis. 

Furthermore, in chapter 15 Deutsch claims that "we can deduce without historical research that every society on Earth before the current Western civilization has either been static or has been destroyed within a few generations."[[5]](#ref5) This rests on his narrow definition of progress as advances in physical explanations and technology. A civilization that made significant strides in urban planning, governance, or agriculture would still be labeled "static" because these fields develop through tinkering and trial-and-error rather than explanatory frameworks. 

## Section 2 closing thoughts

These contradictions reveal a deeper methodological problem: Deutsch treats historical examples as illustrative rather than archival, selecting evidence that supports his thesis while avoiding the constraints of historical rigor. There is a fundamental confusion about what kind of argument Deutsch is making. Is he offering an empirical theory about historical patterns, or a normative framework for thinking about progress? He wants the authority of the first while enjoying the flexibility of the second. When confronted with counterexamples like sophisticated Indigenous political theory or the Achaemenid Empire's tolerance, he can either retrofit them as "mini-enlightenments" or dismiss them as irrelevant to his schema. But you cannot simultaneously use history to ground a normative epistemology and evade the standards of historical accuracy. To explore the deeper pattern in these maneuvers, I propose the lens of an ideological attractor state, to which all evidence must eventually conform. 

# Section 3: The Enlightenment Attractor State

In complex systems theory, an attractor state is a configuration toward which a system naturally evolves and around which it tends to remain stable. Deutsch's framework functions as a conceptual attractor: all cultural progress, intellectual achievement, and moral advancement gets pulled toward and reinterpreted through his specific definition of "Enlightenment values." This creates a kind of ideological topology where every peak of human achievement must be mapped as either a precursor to, manifestation of, or deviation from his Western rationalist ideal.

This attractor effect operates through several mechanisms. First, definitional elasticity: Deutsch's conception of "the Enlightenment" can expand to encompass any achievement he wants to claim (Renaissance Italy, ancient Athens, even individual thinkers like Babbage and Lovelace) while excluding any that don't fit his narrative. Second, retrospective convergence: all genuine progress gets reinterpreted as moving toward his epistemic ideal, while alternative paths are dismissed as dead ends or "mini-enlightenments" that were "tragically snuffed out." Third, normative universalization: what begins as a descriptive account of one particular cultural tradition becomes a prescriptive template for all human societies.

The three mechanisms above transform what should be a "hard-to-vary" explanation into something much more problematic: a "hard-to-escape" conceptual trap. His framework can only interpret diversity as either primitive (pre-Enlightenment) or aberrant (failed enlightenment). Deutsch constructs a story running from Athens through Renaissance Italy to modern science, achieving coherence by systematically ignoring alternative trajectories and retroactively redefining concepts. 

Thus, Deutsch's attractor model contributes to the lack of dialectic around The Beginning of Infinity. It creates a barrier of entry for alternate explanations or critiques of the work, since they would first need to avoid slipping into the attractor. Ironically, a framework that claims to champion rigorous criticism actually creates intellectual conditions that make it nearly impossible. The attractor doesn't just distort our understanding of the past - it constrains our thinking about alternatives in the present. When all genuine progress must be filtered through Enlightenment categories, we lose the capacity to recognize or develop other forms of knowledge, other ways of organizing society, other paths to human flourishing. 

# Conclusion

Deutsch's book gets its name from an Asimov book called The End of Eternity, which is about the problems arising from the editing of history with the primary goal of maximizing the safety of humanity. But in my view, it is the manipulation of history itself, regardless of the purpose, that collapses the future into one. The future opens up infinite possibilities only when you stop manipulating history (destroy the kettles).

The path forward requires recognizing that human creativity and problem-solving capacity are far more diverse and unpredictable than any single framework can contain. We need explanatory frameworks that can account for emergence, non-linearity, and genuine cultural diversity without collapsing into mechanistic reductionism. 

Deutsch's own epistemological principles, properly applied, point toward this conclusion. If we take seriously the idea that knowledge grows through conjecture and refutation, then we must preserve the conditions that allow continued conjecture. The Enlightenment attractor state threatens to close off entire regions of possibility space before they can be explored. The future remains infinite not because we've found the right universal framework, but because we've resisted the temptation to collapse solution space into a single attractor state.

It escapes me why Deutsch's audience spends time defending Deutsch, when the true measure of a fan would be to critique him as heavily as possible, thus advancing the tradition of criticism. If so, this critique represents not a rejection of that tradition but its faithful application. The dialectic remains open and the future remains infinite.
